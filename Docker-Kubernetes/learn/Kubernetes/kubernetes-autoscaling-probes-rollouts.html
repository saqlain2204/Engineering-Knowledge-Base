Autoscaling, Probes & Rollouts

An app in Kubernetes has to cope with three kinds of change: traffic goes up and down, code changes with every release, and pods die for many reasons. This page explains the tools that handle each one: probes (is this pod healthy?), rolling updates (swap versions without downtime), and autoscalers (add or remove pods and machines). It goes deeper than the short sections on probes and HPA in Workloads, and every number is worked out step by step.

3. Requests: the Number Everything Depends On

Every container can say how much CPU and memory it needs, its request (see Resource Requests & Limits). Three tools on this page read that number:

  • The scheduler only places a pod on a node with that much unclaimed room.
  • The HPA measures CPU as a percentage of the request. A pod with request 500m that uses 300m is at 60% utilization.
  • The Cluster Autoscaler adds a node when pods do not fit by request, and removes nodes whose pods ask for little.

Intuition. Requests are a reservation, like booking seats in a restaurant. The scheduler counts booked seats, not people actually eating. If you book too few seats, the HPA thinks every pod is overloaded. If you book too many, nodes look full while the CPUs sit idle, and you pay for machines you do not use.

Without a CPU request, the HPA cannot compute a CPU percentage at all, and it reports an error instead of scaling.

4. Probes: Startup, Readiness, Liveness

The kubelet runs each probe every periodSeconds. A probe "fails" if the HTTP status is not 200-399, the TCP connect fails, the command exits non-zero, or there is no answer within timeoutSeconds. After failureThreshold failures in a row, the probe's action happens. After successThreshold successes (usually 1), it counts as passing again.

ProbeWhen it fails enough timesUse it for
startupProbeThe container is restarted. While it has not yet passed, readiness and liveness are not run.Slow starters: loading a large model, warming a cache, running migrations
readinessProbeThe pod is removed from the Service endpoints. It is not restarted. When it passes again, it is added back."Can I take traffic right now?" Also during shutdown and overload.
livenessProbeThe container is restarted.A process that is running but stuck forever (a deadlock, see Concurrency & Deadlocks)

Timing math

The time to notice a problem is roughly periodSeconds × failureThreshold.

Worked example. Our checkout app (manifest in section 13) uses:

startupProbe:   periodSeconds: 5,  failureThreshold: 24
readinessProbe: periodSeconds: 5,  failureThreshold: 2
livenessProbe:  periodSeconds: 10, failureThreshold: 3
  • Start-up budget: 5 × 24 = 120 seconds. If the app is not healthy after about 2 minutes, it is restarted. Before then, the liveness probe cannot kill it for being slow.
  • Out of traffic: if /ready starts failing, the pod leaves the endpoints after about 5 × 2 = 10 seconds.
  • Restart if stuck: about 10 × 3 = 30 seconds of failed /healthz before a restart.

Why not just give the liveness probe a long initialDelaySeconds instead of a startup probe? Because start-up time varies. A delay of 120 seconds would slow down detection of a stuck app forever; a startup probe only applies once, at the start.

What should each endpoint check?

  • Liveness (/healthz): only "is this process able to respond at all?" Do not check the database here. If the database is down, restarting every pod does not fix it, and all pods restarting at once makes things much worse (they all reconnect at the same moment).
  • Readiness (/ready): "can I do useful work?" It is fine to check things that make this pod useless (for example, "my local cache is loaded"). Be careful with shared dependencies: if every pod's readiness checks the same database, a database blip removes all pods, and users get "no healthy upstream" instead of a slower answer.

5. Rolling Updates, Step by Step

When you change a Deployment's pod template (for example, a new image tag), it creates a new ReplicaSet and moves pods over in steps. Two settings control the speed:

  • maxSurge: how many pods above the desired count may exist during the update.
  • maxUnavailable: how many pods below the desired count may be not-ready during the update.

Both can be a number or a percentage. Percentages are turned into numbers this way: maxSurge rounds up, maxUnavailable rounds down. The default is 25% for both.

Worked example 1: defaults, 10 replicas

maxSurge = 25% of 10 = 2.5, rounded up = 3. maxUnavailable = 2.5, rounded down = 2. So during the update there are at most 10 + 3 = 13 pods, and at least 10 - 2 = 8 ready pods.

Worked example 2: our checkout app, 4 replicas, maxSurge 1, maxUnavailable 0

Never fewer than 4 ready pods, never more than 5 pods. Each step:

step  old (v1)   new (v2)        total   ready   what happens
  0   ● ● ● ●                     4       4      start
  1   ● ● ● ●    ○                5       4      create 1 new (surge = 1)
  2   ● ● ● ●    ●                5       5      new pod passes readiness + minReadySeconds
  3   ● ● ●      ●                4       4      delete 1 old
  4   ● ● ●      ● ○              5       4      create 1 new
 ...  (repeat)
  12             ● ● ● ●          4       4      done
 ● = ready   ○ = starting

If a new pod takes 40 seconds to start plus minReadySeconds: 10, each of the 4 steps takes about 50 seconds, so the rollout takes about 4 × 50 = 200 seconds. With maxSurge: 2 it would do two at a time and take about half as long, at the cost of 2 extra pods' worth of room.

minReadySeconds says "a new pod must stay ready this long before it counts as available". It catches apps that pass readiness and then crash a few seconds later. progressDeadlineSeconds (default 600) marks the rollout as failed if it makes no progress for that long. Kubernetes does not roll back by itself; your pipeline must notice the failed status and run kubectl rollout undo.

kubectl rollout status deployment/checkout     # waits; exits non-zero if it fails
kubectl rollout history deployment/checkout    # list revisions
kubectl rollout undo deployment/checkout       # go back one revision
kubectl rollout pause / resume deployment/checkout

The key safety rule: a rolling update is only as safe as the readiness probe. Without one, a pod counts as ready the moment its container starts, so Kubernetes happily deletes old pods while new ones cannot serve yet. For canary and blue-green releases, see CI/CD and MLOps.

6. How a Pod Shuts Down

Every rollout, scale-down, and node drain ends with pods being deleted. What happens next decides whether users see errors. Two things start at the same time:

kubectl delete pod / rollout / scale down
        │
        ├──► (A) kubelet: run preStop hook (if any), then send SIGTERM
        │        wait up to terminationGracePeriodSeconds (default 30 s)
        │        still running? send SIGKILL
        │
        └──► (B) endpoints controller: remove the pod from the Service
                 → kube-proxy on every node, ingress controllers, and
                   cloud load balancers each update... a little later

The problem is the word "later". Path (B) takes time to reach every node and load balancer. If the app exits the moment it gets SIGTERM, some traffic is still being sent to it, and those requests fail.

The fix has two parts:

  1. A preStop sleep of a few seconds (lifecycle.preStop.sleep.seconds, available from Kubernetes 1.30; on older clusters use an exec hook that runs sleep). The app keeps serving while the rest of the cluster catches up.
  2. Graceful shutdown in the app: on SIGTERM, stop accepting new connections, finish the requests already in progress, close database connections, then exit.

The preStop time counts as part of the grace period, so terminationGracePeriodSeconds must be bigger than the preStop sleep plus your longest request. Our manifest uses a 10 s sleep and a 45 s grace period.

We measured what each piece is worth in a local experiment: Scenario: Errors During Every Deploy. With no readiness check and no graceful shutdown, 30.6% to 30.7% of requests failed during the rollout (three runs). With a readiness check, graceful shutdown and a preStop-style delay, 0 failed in all three runs.

7. The Horizontal Pod Autoscaler (HPA)

The HPA changes a Deployment's replica count based on a measurement. About every 15 seconds it asks the metrics API for the current value, and computes:

desired replicas = ceil( current replicas × current value / target value )

"ceil" means "round up to a whole number". Then it clamps the result between minReplicas and maxReplicas. If the ratio current / target is within 10% of 1.0 (the default tolerance), it does nothing, so it does not flap up and down over tiny changes.

Worked examples, checked with code

This short Python function is the same formula. We ran it with minReplicas = 4 and maxReplicas = 20:

import math

def desired(current_pods, current_value, target_value,
            min_pods=1, max_pods=100, tolerance=0.1):
    ratio = current_value / target_value
    if abs(ratio - 1.0) <= tolerance:          # close enough: do nothing
        return current_pods
    want = math.ceil(current_pods * ratio)     # always round UP
    return max(min_pods, min(max_pods, want))
busy: 4 pods at 90% CPU, target 60%              -> 6 pods
almost on target: 6 pods at 63%                  -> 6 pods
quiet: 10 pods at 20%                            -> 4 pods
spike: 4 pods at 300%                            -> 20 pods
requests: 5 pods at 180 req/s each, target 100   -> 9 pods
  • Busy: 4 × 90 / 60 = 6.0, so 6.
  • Almost on target: 63 / 60 = 1.05, within the 10% tolerance, so no change.
  • Quiet: 10 × 20 / 60 = 3.33, rounded up to 4, which is also the minimum.
  • Spike: 4 × 300 / 60 = 20. (CPU can go above 100% of the request when there is no limit or the limit is higher.) The formula says 20, but scaling speed rules (next section) may get there in steps.
  • Requests per second: 5 × 180 / 100 = 9. Here the metric is not CPU but a custom one.

Which metric?

Metric typeExampleNeeds
Resource (CPU, memory)Average CPU at 60% of requestmetrics-server installed in the cluster
Pods (custom, per pod)Requests per second per pod, queue length per workerA custom metrics adapter, for example Prometheus Adapter
ExternalMessages waiting in a cloud queueAn external metrics adapter; KEDA is a popular tool that also scales to zero

If you list several metrics, the HPA computes a count for each and uses the largest. Memory is often a poor scaling metric: many apps (Java, Python with caches) do not give memory back when load drops, so the HPA scales up and never down.

CPU works well for apps where CPU rises with load. For apps that wait on other services (most web APIs) or work through a queue, requests in flight per pod or queue length per worker usually track load better. (Little's law, from Debugging a Slow API, links requests per second, time per request, and requests in flight.)

8. Scaling Speed: Behavior and Stabilization

The formula says where to go. The behavior section says how fast.

  • stabilizationWindowSeconds: the HPA looks at all the counts it computed over this window and, when scaling down, picks the highest one. So a short dip in traffic does not remove pods. Default: 300 seconds (5 minutes) for scale-down, 0 for scale-up.
  • policies: limits like "add at most 100% per 60 s" or "remove at most 2 pods per 60 s". Default for scale-up: in each 15 s, add up to 100% or 4 pods, whichever is more.

Worked example: scale-up limited to doubling each minute

Our HPA allows +100% per 60 s. Traffic jumps so the formula says 20 pods, starting from 4:

minute 0:  4 pods   → may go to 4 × 2 = 8
minute 1:  8 pods   → may go to 16
minute 2: 16 pods   → may go to 32, but max is 20 → 20

About 2 minutes to reach 20 (plus the time for new pods to start).

Worked example: careful scale-down

Traffic drops at 14:00 IST. The formula says 4 pods, from 12. The 5-minute window means nothing happens until about 14:05. Then the policy removes at most 2 pods per minute: 12 → 10 → 8 → 6 → 4, which takes 4 more minutes. Fully scaled down at about 14:09. Slow on purpose: removing pods too fast and then needing them again is worse than paying for a few extra minutes.

9. The Vertical Pod Autoscaler (VPA)

The HPA adds more pods. The VPA changes how big each pod is: it watches real CPU and memory use over time and recommends better requests. It is an add-on (not built in) with three parts: a recommender that computes the numbers, an updater that evicts pods whose requests are far off, and an admission controller that writes the new requests into pods as they are created.

updateModeWhat it does
OffOnly recommends. You read the numbers and change the manifest yourself. Safest start.
InitialSets requests only when a pod is created; never evicts.
Recreate / AutoEvicts and recreates pods to apply new requests. Newer VPA versions can also try to resize pods in place, using Kubernetes' in-place resize feature.
 
kubectl describe vpa report-worker   # look for: Recommendation → Target, Lower Bound, Upper Bound

Do not let the HPA and VPA both act on CPU for the same Deployment. The VPA raises the CPU request, which lowers the CPU percentage, which makes the HPA remove pods, which raises CPU per pod again: they fight. Common pattern: HPA on a custom metric (like requests per second), and VPA in Off mode to help you pick requests.

The VPA is a good fit for things that cannot be split into more copies: a single-instance job, a batch worker with uneven input, or an app whose memory need you do not know yet.

10. Adding Machines: Cluster Autoscaler and Karpenter

The HPA can ask for 20 pods, but if the nodes are full, the extra pods stay Pending. A node autoscaler fixes that.

Cluster Autoscaler

  • Scale up: every few seconds it looks for Pending pods that cannot be scheduled. It asks: "if I added one more node from this node group, would they fit?" If yes, it asks the cloud for more machines in that group.
  • Scale down: a node whose pods' requests add up to less than 50% of its size (the default threshold) for 10 minutes (the default wait), and whose pods can all move elsewhere, is drained and removed.
  • Things that stop a node from being removed: pods with local storage, pods not managed by a controller, a PodDisruptionBudget that would be broken, and the annotation cluster-autoscaler.kubernetes.io/safe-to-evict: "false".

Karpenter

Karpenter (first built for AWS, now also on Azure) does not use fixed node groups. It looks at the Pending pods and picks a machine type that fits them, possibly a different size each time. It also consolidates: it replaces several half-empty nodes with fewer, cheaper ones.

The slow part

A new pod on an existing node can start in seconds. A new node takes much longer: the cloud must boot a machine, it must join the cluster, then pull your image. That usually takes minutes, and large images (like LLM servers, see GPU Scheduling) make it longer. To hide this delay, some teams run low-priority placeholder pods that only reserve space. When real pods need the room, the scheduler evicts the placeholders right away (they have a lower PriorityClass), and the node autoscaler adds a node for the placeholders in the background.

11. PodDisruptionBudgets

Some pod removals are voluntary: a person or tool decided to do them (draining a node for an upgrade, the Cluster Autoscaler removing a node). Others are involuntary: a machine crashes. A PodDisruptionBudget (PDB) limits voluntary ones:

apiVersion: policy/v1
kind: PodDisruptionBudget
metadata:
  name: checkout
spec:
  minAvailable: 3        # or maxUnavailable: 1
  selector:
    matchLabels:
      app: checkout

With 4 checkout pods and minAvailable: 3, a kubectl drain may evict only one checkout pod at a time. It waits until a replacement is ready before evicting the next. PDBs do not protect against crashes, and they do not slow down a Deployment's own rolling update (that is what maxUnavailable is for).

Pitfall: minAvailable equal to the replica count (for example 4 of 4) means no pod can ever be evicted, so node drains hang forever and cluster upgrades get stuck.

12. Putting It Together: a Traffic Spike, Minute by Minute

A shop's checkout service runs 4 pods. Each pod requests 500m CPU. The HPA target is 60%. Each node has 4 CPUs free for these pods, so 8 pods fit per node, and there is one node with room for 2 more. A sale starts at 12:00 IST and traffic triples.

12:00:00  traffic ×3. CPU per pod jumps from 50% to about 150%.
12:00:15  HPA: 4 × 150 / 60 = 10 → policy allows ×2 per minute → 8 pods.
12:00:20  2 new pods fit on the free space → start. 2 pods Pending.
12:00:25  Cluster Autoscaler sees Pending pods → asks the cloud for a node.
12:00:50  first 2 new pods pass readiness → 6 pods serving.
12:01:15  HPA: now 6 ready pods at about 100% → wants 10 → allowed (≤ 16).
12:02:30  new node joined, image pulled → the Pending pods start.
12:03:10  10 pods ready. CPU per pod about 60%. Stable.

(The times here are an illustration of the order of events, not a measurement. Real node start-up time depends on the cloud, the machine type, and the image size.)

What did users see? Between 12:00 and about 12:03, the pods were overloaded, so responses were slow. What would make it better?

  • Scale before the sale: the sale time is known, so raise minReplicas to 10 at 11:45 (a scheduled job, or a tool like KEDA's cron trigger).
  • Lower the target from 60% to 50% to keep more spare room for sudden jumps.
  • Placeholder pods so a node is already waiting.
  • Make start-up faster: smaller images, lazy loading.
  • Protect the app while it scales: limit concurrent requests and return 429 or 503 quickly instead of letting all requests get slow (load shedding, see Debugging a Slow API).

Schema checks catch typos and wrong fields. They do not prove the app behaves well; that needs a real cluster or a test like the one in the deploy scenario.

deploy.yaml

apiVersion: apps/v1
kind: Deployment
metadata:
  name: checkout
spec:
  replicas: 4
  selector:
    matchLabels:
      app: checkout
  strategy:
    type: RollingUpdate
    rollingUpdate:
      maxSurge: 1          # at most 1 extra pod during the update
      maxUnavailable: 0    # never go below 4 ready pods
  minReadySeconds: 10      # a new pod must stay ready 10 s to count
  template:
    metadata:
      labels:
        app: checkout
    spec:
      terminationGracePeriodSeconds: 45
      containers:
        - name: app
          image: registry.example.com/checkout:1.8.2
          ports:
            - containerPort: 8080
          resources:
            requests:
              cpu: 500m
              memory: 256Mi
            limits:
              memory: 512Mi
          startupProbe:
            httpGet:
              path: /healthz
              port: 8080
            periodSeconds: 5
            failureThreshold: 24     # allow up to 24 x 5 = 120 s to start
          readinessProbe:
            httpGet:
              path: /ready
              port: 8080
            periodSeconds: 5
            failureThreshold: 2
          livenessProbe:
            httpGet:
              path: /healthz
              port: 8080
            periodSeconds: 10
            failureThreshold: 3
          lifecycle:
            preStop:
              sleep:
                seconds: 10          # keep serving while load balancers catch up

Note there is a memory limit but no CPU limit. A CPU limit causes throttling (the container is paused for part of every 100 ms period even when the node has idle CPU; see CPU Scheduling), which shows up as slow requests. Many teams set CPU requests and skip CPU limits for latency-sensitive services. Memory limits stay, because memory cannot be throttled, only killed.

hpa.yaml

apiVersion: autoscaling/v2
kind: HorizontalPodAutoscaler
metadata:
  name: checkout
spec:
  scaleTargetRef:
    apiVersion: apps/v1
    kind: Deployment
    name: checkout
  minReplicas: 4
  maxReplicas: 20
  metrics:
    - type: Resource
      resource:
        name: cpu
        target:
          type: Utilization
          averageUtilization: 60     # aim for 60% of the CPU request
  behavior:
    scaleUp:
      stabilizationWindowSeconds: 0
      policies:
        - type: Percent
          value: 100                 # at most double every 60 s
          periodSeconds: 60
    scaleDown:
      stabilizationWindowSeconds: 300
      policies:
        - type: Pods
          value: 2                   # remove at most 2 pods per minute
          periodSeconds: 60

When an HPA manages a Deployment, remove replicas: from the Deployment you apply with GitOps, or every sync will reset the count back to 4 and fight the HPA.

vpa.yaml

apiVersion: autoscaling.k8s.io/v1
kind: VerticalPodAutoscaler
metadata:
  name: report-worker
spec:
  targetRef:
    apiVersion: apps/v1
    kind: Deployment
    name: report-worker
  updatePolicy:
    updateMode: "Off"        # only recommend; do not restart pods

14. Common Pitfalls

  • No readiness probe. Pods get traffic before they can serve, and rollouts delete old pods too early.
  • Liveness checks a shared dependency. The database blips and every pod restarts at once.
  • Liveness with no startup probe on a slow app. The app is killed while still loading, restarts, is killed again: a CrashLoopBackOff that only happens in production, where data is bigger.
  • Exiting at once on SIGTERM. Requests in flight are cut, and new ones still arrive for a moment. Add a preStop sleep and graceful shutdown.
  • No CPU request on an HPA target. The HPA cannot compute utilization.
  • replicas: in Git plus an HPA. GitOps keeps resetting the count.
  • HPA and VPA both on CPU. They fight.
  • maxReplicas too low, or not enough node quota in the cloud account. The autoscaler silently stops at the ceiling.
  • Scaling on memory for apps that never free memory. Scales up, never down.
  • PDB with minAvailable = replicas. Drains and upgrades hang.
  • Forgetting the slow path. A new node takes minutes. Plan for known spikes ahead of time.

15. Practice Problems

Work these out first. Answers follow each one.

Problem 1: HPA math

8 pods, CPU target 70%, current average 105%. minReplicas 2, maxReplicas 10. What does the HPA want?

Answer: 105 / 70 = 1.5, outside the tolerance. 8 × 1.5 = 12, capped at maxReplicas: 10.

Problem 2: tolerance

5 pods, target 50%, current 54%. What happens? What if current is 56%?

Answer: 54 / 50 = 1.08, within 10%: no change. 56 / 50 = 1.12: 5 × 1.12 = 5.6, round up: 6.

Problem 3: rolling update limits

A Deployment has 8 replicas with maxSurge 25% and maxUnavailable 25%. What is the most pods there can be, and the fewest ready pods?

Answer: 25% of 8 = 2 exactly, so maxSurge = 2 and maxUnavailable = 2. At most 10 pods; at least 6 ready.

Problem 4: rounding

Same settings but 6 replicas.

Answer: 25% of 6 = 1.5. maxSurge rounds up to 2, maxUnavailable rounds down to 1. At most 8 pods; at least 5 ready.

Problem 5: probe timing

A model server takes between 60 and 150 seconds to load. Design a startup probe with periodSeconds 10. How large must failureThreshold be? Then the liveness probe should restart it within about 30 seconds of getting stuck. Suggest settings.

Answer: The start-up budget must exceed 150 s with some margin: 10 × 18 = 180 s, so failureThreshold: 18. Liveness: periodSeconds: 10, failureThreshold: 3 (about 30 s).

Problem 6: shutdown budget

Your preStop sleep is 15 s. The slowest normal request takes 20 s. Is terminationGracePeriodSeconds: 30 enough?

Answer: No. The preStop time counts inside the grace period: 15 + 20 = 35 s > 30 s, so a slow request could be killed by SIGKILL. Use at least 40 (some margin).

Problem 7: why is it Pending?

The HPA scaled to 12 pods, but 3 stay Pending for 20 minutes. The Cluster Autoscaler is installed. Name three things to check.

Answer: (1) kubectl describe pod events: is it CPU, memory, a node selector, or a taint that no node group can satisfy? (2) Has the node group reached its maximum size, or has the cloud account hit its quota? (3) The Cluster Autoscaler's own logs or status (for example its "NotTriggerScaleUp" events): it explains why no node group would help. Also check whether a single pod requests more than any node type offers.

Problem 8: scale-down time

Using the HPA in section 13: 20 pods, traffic drops so the formula says 4. About how long until 4?

Answer: 5 minutes of stabilization, then at most 2 pods per minute: 16 pods to remove = 8 minutes. About 13 minutes in total.
