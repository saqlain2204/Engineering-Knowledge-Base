Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)

Grounding LLM answers in your own documents. We build a small RAG retriever for real, compare keyword search, embedding search, hybrid search, and reranking on the same questions, and learn to debug the failures. All results on this page come from the code shown.

1. Why RAG Exists

An LLM only knows what was in its training data. It does not know your company's help docs, last week's policy change, or a private contract. It also remembers facts imperfectly, and may make up an answer that sounds right. This is called hallucination.

RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) fixes this at question time: first retrieve the passages that are relevant to the question, then put them in the prompt, and ask the model to answer from those passages.

Intuition: RAG turns a closed-book exam into an open-book exam. The student (the LLM) is still the one writing the answer, but now it can look things up. If the wrong page is opened, the answer will still be wrong, so finding the right page is most of the work.

Benefits: fewer made-up facts, access to private data without retraining, and easy updates (change the documents, not the model). RAG does not guarantee truth. The model can still ignore or misread the passages.

2. The Big Picture

Words used below:

  • Chunk: a small piece of a document, a few sentences to a few paragraphs. We retrieve chunks, not whole documents.
  • Index: a data structure that makes searching fast.
  • Top-k: the k best results. "Top-5" means the five highest-scoring chunks.
  • Embedding: a list of numbers that represents the meaning of a text. Similar meanings give nearby lists. See Embeddings.

3. Ingestion and Chunking

Documents flow through: parsing (PDF, HTML, DOCX, tickets into plain text, keeping headings), cleaning (remove menus, footers, duplicates), chunking, attaching metadata (title, URL, section, date, who may see it), embedding, and indexing.

Why chunk at all?

  • An embedding of a 30-page document is a blurry average of everything in it. A small chunk has a sharp meaning.
  • The prompt has limited room. Small chunks let you include only the relevant parts.

Chunking, seen in a real run

A 30-word help text, split into chunks of 10 words:

size=10 overlap=0
  chunk 0: To reset your password, open Settings and choose Security. Click
  chunk 1: Reset password. We email you a link. The link expires
  chunk 2: after 30 minutes. If it expires, request a new one.
size=10 overlap=3
  chunk 0: To reset your password, open Settings and choose Security. Click
  chunk 1: choose Security. Click Reset password. We email you a link.
  chunk 2: you a link. The link expires after 30 minutes. If
  chunk 3: 30 minutes. If it expires, request a new one.
  chunk 4: new one.

What to notice:

  • Without overlap, "The link expires" and "after 30 minutes" landed in different chunks. A question like "how long is the reset link valid?" may now match neither chunk well.
  • Overlap repeats the last few words of each chunk at the start of the next, so ideas that cross a boundary survive in at least one chunk.
  • Overlap has a cost: more chunks to store and search, and duplicates in results. It also made a useless tail chunk, "new one." Real chunkers merge tiny tails into the previous chunk.

Better chunking strategies

StrategyHowGood for
Fixed size + overlapEvery N tokens, overlap MQuick start, plain text
Structure-awareSplit on headings, paragraphs, list items; never mid-sentenceDocs, manuals, web pages
Parent-childSearch over small chunks, but send the larger parent section to the LLMPrecise search plus enough context
SemanticStart a new chunk where the topic changes (embedding similarity drops)Long mixed documents

Common starting sizes are a few hundred tokens. Measure with your own questions (section 10) instead of trusting any one number.

4. Keyword Search: BM25 by Hand

BM25 is the classic search-engine scoring formula. It scores a chunk higher when:

  1. The query words appear in it, more times is better, but with diminishing returns (the 10th mention adds little).
  2. The words are rare across all chunks. Matching "ERR-4012" means much more than matching "page". This is measured by IDF (inverse document frequency).
  3. The chunk is short. One match in a short chunk is stronger evidence than one match in a long one.

For a query word \(t\), with \(N\) chunks, of which \(n_t\) contain the word:

\[ \text{IDF}(t) = \ln\!\left(\frac{N - n_t + 0.5}{n_t + 0.5} + 1\right) \]

\[ \text{score} = \sum_{t \in \text{query}} \text{IDF}(t)\cdot\frac{f_t\,(k_1 + 1)}{f_t + k_1\left(1 - b + b\,\frac{\text{len}}{\text{avglen}}\right)} \]

\(f_t\) is how many times the word appears in this chunk. \(k_1\) (usually 1.2 to 2) controls the diminishing returns. \(b\) (usually 0.75) controls the length adjustment. We used \(k_1 = 1.5, b = 0.75\).

Worked IDF numbers from our experiment

Our corpus has \(N = 12\) chunks (listed in section 6).

  • "err-4012" is in 1 chunk: \(\ln(11.5/1.5 + 1) = \ln(8.667) = 2.159\).
  • "billing" is in 2 chunks: IDF = 1.649.
  • "api" is in 3 chunks: \(\ln(9.5/3.5 + 1) = \ln(3.714) = 1.312\).

Rarer word, bigger weight. That is exactly what you want for error codes, product names, and IDs.

Big weakness: BM25 only matches the same words. "I forgot my login credentials" shares no word with "To reset your password..." so BM25 scores it 0.

5. Meaning Search: Dense Embeddings

An embedding model turns each chunk into a vector (we used all-MiniLM-L6-v2, which makes 384 numbers per text). The question is embedded the same way. The score is the cosine similarity: how closely the two vectors point in the same direction, from -1 to 1, higher is more similar.

Because the model was trained on many pairs of related texts, "forgot my login credentials" and "reset your password" end up close together even with no shared words. This is called dense retrieval (dense because every one of the 384 numbers is used, unlike a keyword index where most entries are zero).

Weakness: exact strings. Codes like ERR-4012 and ERR-4013 look almost the same to an embedding model, even though they mean different things. At scale, a vector database finds the nearest vectors quickly with approximate search.

What we learn

  • BM25 found nothing at all for 4 of 10 questions, and the wrong chunk for a fifth. Users said "bills" and the doc said "invoices", "2FA" versus "Two-factor authentication", and so on. For "stop paying for the service" it matched the word "service" in the refund chunk and returned the wrong answer.
  • Dense search ranked every gold chunk first on this small, paraphrase-heavy set.
  • But look at the margins. For "ERR-4013", dense gave the right chunk D11 a similarity of 0.583 and the wrong one, D3 (ERR-4012), 0.545. Very close. BM25 matched D11 only, because "err-4013" is a different word from "err-4012". With thousands of error codes, that thin margin becomes wrong answers.
  • This is a tiny test made to teach. On real data, which method wins depends on how your users write. Measure on your own questions.

The code

import math, re
from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer   # sentence-transformers 6.3.0

tok = lambda s: re.findall(r"[a-z0-9]+(?:-[0-9]+)?", s.lower())
STOP = set("a an the to of and or is in on at your you my i do how what can it "
           "for from as if be this are with get by".split())
ids = list(docs)                                   # docs = {"D1": "...", ...}
toks = {d: [t for t in tok(docs[d]) if t not in STOP] for d in ids}
N = len(ids); avgdl = sum(map(len, toks.values())) / N
df = {}
for words in toks.values():
    for t in set(words): df[t] = df.get(t, 0) + 1
idf = lambda t: math.log((N - df.get(t, 0) + 0.5) / (df.get(t, 0) + 0.5) + 1)

def bm25(q, k1=1.5, b=0.75):
    qt = [t for t in tok(q) if t not in STOP]
    out = {}
    for d in ids:
        s = 0.0
        for t in qt:
            f = toks[d].count(t)
            if f:
                s += idf(t) * f * (k1 + 1) / (f + k1 * (1 - b + b * len(toks[d]) / avgdl))
        out[d] = s
    return out

model = SentenceTransformer("sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2")
E = model.encode([docs[d] for d in ids], normalize_embeddings=True)
def dense(q):
    v = model.encode([q], normalize_embeddings=True)[0]
    return dict(zip(ids, (E @ v).tolist()))      # cosine similarity

def ranking(scores):                             # drop chunks with no match at all
    return [d for d in sorted(ids, key=lambda d: -scores[d]) if scores[d] > 0]

7. Hybrid Search and Reciprocal Rank Fusion

Keyword and dense search fail in different ways, so production systems often run both and merge the lists. This is hybrid search. The scores are on different scales (BM25 6.4 versus cosine 0.67), so instead of adding scores, a simple and popular method merges ranks: Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF).

\[ \text{RRF}(d) = \sum_{\text{each list}} \frac{1}{k + \text{rank of } d \text{ in that list}} \qquad (k = 60 \text{ is the usual choice}) \]

def rrf(rankings, k=60):
    out = {d: 0.0 for d in ids}
    for r in rankings:
        for i, d in enumerate(r):
            out[d] += 1 / (k + i + 1)
    return out

Worked example: where hybrid went wrong

Question: "how do I stop paying for the service". Correct chunk: D4.

  • BM25 list: [D9] (it matched "service").
  • Dense list: [D4, D9, D6, ...].
ChunkFrom BM25From denseRRF total
D91/(60+1) = 0.0163931/(60+2) = 0.0161290.032522
D4not in list: 01/(60+1) = 0.0163930.016393
D601/(60+3) = 0.0158730.015873

D9 appears in both lists, so it wins, even though it is the wrong answer. Over the 10 questions, hybrid was in between: recall@1 was 0.9 (BM25 0.5, dense 1.0). The right answer was still in hybrid's top 3 every time, which is why the next stage, reranking, exists.

Lesson: hybrid search is mainly a recall tool: it makes sure the right chunk is somewhere in the candidate list. Ordering the list well is the reranker's job.

8. Reranking with a Cross-Encoder

The embedding model encodes the question and each chunk separately, so it is fast enough to search millions of chunks. A cross-encoder reads the question and one chunk together and outputs a relevance score. It is more accurate but much slower, so it is only used on the top 20 to 100 candidates.

We ran the open cross-encoder/ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2 model on five candidates per question: hybrid search's top 3 plus two other chunks (scores are raw model outputs; only the order matters):

how do I stop paying for the service
  D4: -2.505      <- correct chunk, now first
  D9: -5.804
  D6: -10.542
  D8: -10.705
  D1: -11.260
can I get my money back
  D8: -10.279
  D4: -10.367
  D9: -10.676     <- correct chunk, pushed down to third
  D2: -11.109
  D6: -11.324
  • For the first question the reranker fixed the hybrid mistake: D4 is now first by a wide margin.
  • For the second, it made things worse: dense and hybrid had D9 first, the reranker put it third. All five scores are very low and close together, which means the model is unsure. This reranker was trained on web search queries (the MS MARCO dataset), not casual support questions.

So rerankers usually help, but not always. Test them on your data, and consider a reranker trained on data like yours.

from sentence_transformers import CrossEncoder
ce = CrossEncoder("cross-encoder/ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2")

9. Generation: Grounded Prompts

The retrieved chunks go into the prompt with clear labels, and the instructions require the model to stay grounded:

SYSTEM:
You are a support assistant. Answer ONLY from the sources below.
Cite the source ID in brackets after each fact, like [D4].
If the sources do not contain the answer, say "I don't know based on our docs."

SOURCES:
[D4] You can cancel your subscription at any time from the Billing page.
     Access continues until the end of the paid period.
[D9] Refunds are given within 14 days of a charge if the service was not used.

USER:
how do I stop paying for the service

Guidelines:

  • Fit the context budget. Put the highest-ranked chunks first, remove near-duplicates.
  • Mind the middle. Research found that models use information at the start and end of a long context better than information in the middle ("lost in the middle"). Do not bury the best chunk in the middle of 30 others.
  • Allow "I don't know". Without it, the model fills gaps by guessing.
  • Filter by permissions before retrieval, not after. Never let a user's question pull in documents they are not allowed to see.

10. Measuring RAG

Measure the two halves separately. If you only measure the final answer, you cannot tell whether search or the LLM failed.

Retrieval metrics

  • Recall@k: share of questions where the correct chunk is in the top k. Our results: BM25 recall@3 = 0.5, dense = 1.0, hybrid = 1.0.
  • MRR (mean reciprocal rank): for each question take 1 / (rank of the correct chunk), or 0 if not found, then average. Rewards putting it near the top. Our results: BM25 0.5, dense 1.0, hybrid 0.95.

Check hybrid's MRR by hand: nine questions at rank 1 give 1 each; one question at rank 2 gives 0.5. (9 + 0.5) / 10 = 0.95.

Answer metrics

  • Faithfulness: is every claim in the answer supported by the retrieved chunks?
  • Answer relevance / correctness: does it actually answer the question, compared with a reference answer?
  • Citation accuracy: do the cited chunks really contain the cited facts?

These are usually scored with an LLM judge plus human spot checks. See Evaluation for golden sets, judges, and CI gates.

Operations

  • Log the retrieved chunk IDs with every answer. When a user reports a bad answer, you can see whether search or generation failed.
  • Version the embedding model and the index together. Mixing vectors from two embedding models in one index silently breaks search.
  • Re-index when documents change, and on a schedule.

11. Advanced Patterns

  • Query rewriting: an LLM rewrites a vague question ("it's not working") into a search-friendly one, or splits a complex question into sub-questions. HyDE asks the LLM to write a fake answer first and searches with that.
  • Agentic RAG: the LLM decides when to search, what to search for, and whether to search again. See Agents.
  • Graph RAG: build a knowledge graph of entities and links across documents, for questions that need several hops ("which teams use the service that failed yesterday?").
  • Corrective RAG (CRAG): a grader checks whether the retrieved chunks are relevant; if not, it searches again or falls back to another source.
  • Long-context models vs RAG: even with very long context windows, RAG is still useful for cost, speed, freshness, and permissions. The two are often combined.

12. Scenario: "The Bot Says It Does Not Know"

Situation. Users ask "How long is the password reset link valid?" The bot answers "I don't know based on our docs." But the docs clearly say "The link expires after 30 minutes."

Step 1: split the problem

Open the logged chunk IDs for that answer. Was the correct chunk retrieved?

  • If yes, the problem is generation: prompt, context order, or the model. Go to step 4.
  • If no, the problem is retrieval. Continue.

Step 2: search for the chunk yourself

Find which chunk contains "expires after 30 minutes". You discover the chunker split the sentence: one chunk ends with "The link expires", the next begins "after 30 minutes" (exactly what happened in section 3 without overlap). Neither half matches the question well.

Step 3: fix retrieval

  1. Switch to sentence- and heading-aware chunking so sentences are never cut.
  2. Add overlap.
  3. Add the section title to each chunk ("Password reset: ..."), which helps both BM25 and embeddings.
  4. Add this question to your eval set, re-index, and check recall@5 went up without hurting other questions.

Step 4: if retrieval was fine

  • Was the chunk cut off by the context budget? Check the final prompt that was sent.
  • Was it buried in the middle of many chunks? Rerank and send fewer.
  • Is the "say I don't know" instruction too strong? Test a softer version on your eval set.

13. Common Pitfalls

  • Tuning prompts when retrieval is the problem. Check recall@k first. Most RAG failures are retrieval failures.
  • Cutting sentences in half with naive fixed-size chunks.
  • Dense-only search for codes, IDs, and names. Add BM25 (hybrid).
  • No eval set. Without 50 to 200 real questions with known answers, every change is a guess.
  • Stale index. Docs changed, index did not.
  • Permissions applied after retrieval, or not at all.

14. Practice Problems (with Solutions)

Problem 1: IDF

A corpus has 100 chunks. "kubernetes" is in 50 of them, "kubelet" in 1. Compute the IDF of each with the formula in section 4.

Solution

kubernetes: ln((100 - 50 + 0.5)/(50 + 0.5) + 1) = ln(1 + 1) = ln 2 = 0.693. kubelet: ln((100 - 1 + 0.5)/(1 + 0.5) + 1) = ln(66.33 + 1) = ln(67.33) = 4.21. A match on "kubelet" counts about 6 times more.

Problem 2: RRF

BM25 returns [A, B, C]. Dense returns [C, A, D]. With k = 60, what is the fused order?

Solution

A: 1/61 + 1/62 = 0.016393 + 0.016129 = 0.032522. C: 1/63 + 1/61 = 0.015873 + 0.016393 = 0.032266. B: 1/62 = 0.016129. D: 1/63 = 0.015873. Order: A, C, B, D.

Problem 3: recall and MRR

For 4 questions, the correct chunk was at rank 1, 3, not found, and 2. Compute recall@1, recall@3, and MRR.

Solution

recall@1 = 1/4 = 0.25. recall@3 = 3/4 = 0.75 (ranks 1, 3, 2). MRR = (1 + 1/3 + 0 + 1/2)/4 = 1.8333/4 = 0.458.

Problem 4: choose the retriever

You are building search over an internal bug tracker. Users mostly paste error messages and ticket IDs like PAY-2231. What retrieval setup would you start with?

Solution

Hybrid with strong BM25 weight (exact error strings and IDs are critical), dense search to catch paraphrased descriptions, then a reranker. Keep the tokenizer from splitting "PAY-2231" into meaningless pieces, and build an eval set of real pasted errors to confirm.
